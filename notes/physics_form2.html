TOPIC-01: STATIC ELECTRICITY

1.1: The Concept of Static Electricity

  • Static electricity (Electrostatics) is the study of charges at rest.

Origin of Charges

  • When a body is rubbed, it causes the atoms to lose or gain electrons which are revolving around the atomic structure, causing the body to become charged.

Types of Electric Charges

  1. Positive charge (\(+\))
  2. Negative charge (\(-\))
  1. Positive charge

    Positive charge is a charge acquired when an object loses electrons from its atomic structure.

  2. Negative charge

    Negative charge is a charge acquired when an object gains electrons into its atomic structure.

Charge Acquired After Rubbing

Fundamental Law of Electrostatics (Static Charges)

The law states that:

“Like charges repel, unlike charges attract each other.”

Charging

Charging is the process whereby a material loses or gains electrons.

Methods of Charging a Body

  1. By rubbing or friction
  2. By conduction or contact
  3. By induction

By Friction

  • This is the method of charging a body by rubbing.
  • For example, when an ebonite rod is rubbed with fur, some electrons are transferred from the fur to the ebonite. Therefore, the ebonite becomes negatively charged while the fur becomes positively charged.
  • When a glass rod is rubbed with silk, electrons will be transferred from the glass rod to silk; the glass rod will be positively charged while the silk will be negatively charged.

By Contact

  • This is the method of charging a body by touching two conductors together.
  • Let us say plate X is positively charged while plate Y is neutral. Both plates are placed on insulating blocks as shown below:
  • If the two plates are brought together, plate X draws electrons from plate Y, causing plate Y to become positively charged.

By Induction

  • This is the method whereby a charged object is brought near, but not touching, a neutral conducting object.
  1. Consider two bodies: a charged body X and an uncharged body Y.
  2. Place X near Y. Since like charges repel and unlike charges attract, the charged object induces opposite charges on the near side of Y and similar charges on the far side.
  3. Touch Y to allow movement of electrons to the ground. The flow of electrons to the ground is called Earthing.
  4. Remove the earth connection and then remove X; the uncharged body Y becomes charged.

1.2: Detection of Charges

Gold Leaf Electroscope

  • A Gold leaf electroscope is an instrument used for detecting the presence and sign of an electrical charge on an object.
  • It consists of a gold leaf and a brass rod held by insulating materials inside a glass case.
  • The brass cap, brass rod, and gold leaf form the conducting part of the electroscope.

Charging a Gold Leaf Electroscope

  • It can be charged by contact or induction using a charged rod or electrophorus.

Discharging a Gold-Leaf Electroscope

  • It can be discharged effectively by touching the cap with a conductor connected to the earth.

Uses of Gold Leaf Electroscope

  1. To test the sign of charge on a charged body.
  2. Identifying the insulating or conducting properties of materials.
  3. Detecting the presence of charges on a body.
  4. Estimating the quantity of charge on a material.

1.3: Electrophorus

  • An electrophorus is a device used to produce continuous electrostatic charges through electrostatic induction.
  • It consists of a metal disc made of brass fitted with an insulating handle resting on a polythene or rubber base.
  • It can produce numerous positive charges from a single negative charge.

Charging an Electrophorus

  • The polythene base is given a negative charge by rubbing it with fur. Placing the brass plate on top induces a positive charge on its lower surface and a negative charge on its upper surface. Touching the upper surface earths the negative charges, leaving the electrophorus with a net positive charge.

1.4: Conductors and Insulators

Conductors

  • A conductor is a material that allows electric charges to flow through it easily.
  • Examples of conductors include metals such as iron, copper, aluminum, and silver.

Insulators

  • An insulator is a material that does not allow electric charges to flow through it easily.
  • Examples of insulators include plastic, wood, rubber, mica, ebonite, and glass.

1.5: Capacitors

  • A capacitor is a device used to store electric charges and energy.
  • The ability of a capacitor to store electric charges is known as capacitance.
  • The SI unit of capacitance is the Farad (F).
  • A Farad is the capacitance of a capacitor in which a charge of one coulomb produces a potential difference of one volt across its plates.
  • Capacitors are widely used in electronic circuits such as radios, televisions, and alarm systems.
  • The potential difference, \(V\), across the plates is directly proportional to the charge, \(Q\), accumulating on them: \(Q \propto V\).
  • Introducing the capacitance constant \(C\):
    $$Q = CV$$

Charging a Capacitor

  • A parallel plate capacitor consists of two metal plates (plate A and plate B) separated by a dielectric material. The plates accumulate opposite charges when connected across a potential difference.

Important Terms:

  • Dielectric Material: An electrical insulator that can be polarized by an applied electric field.
  • Polarization: The process of separating negative and positive charges in a material.
  • Potential Difference (p.d.): Work done in moving a unit positive charge from one point to another. Its SI unit is the Volt (\(\text{V}\)).
  • Earth Potential: The electrical potential of the Earth is taken as zero.

Discharging a Capacitor

  • When the two plates of a charged capacitor are connected by a wire, electrons flow from the negative plate to neutralize positive charges on the positive plate.
  • This momentary movement of electrons produces a brief pulse of current (and potentially a small spark).
  • When charge flow stops, the capacitor is fully discharged.

Types of Capacitors

Capacitors are classified according to their dielectric material:

  1. Paper (plastic film) capacitor
  2. Oil-filled capacitor
  3. Electrolytic capacitor
  4. Mica capacitor
  5. Variable (air-filled) capacitor

Arrangement of Capacitors

(a) Capacitors in Series Connection

In a series circuit, total potential difference is split across the capacitors: \(V_T = V_1 + V_2\).

Since \(V = \frac{Q}{C}\) and charge \(Q\) is identical across all series capacitors (\(Q_T = Q_1 = Q_2 = Q\)):

$$\frac{Q}{C_T} = \frac{Q}{C_1} + \frac{Q}{C_2}$$

Dividing through by \(Q\) gives the effective capacitance formula:

$$\frac{1}{C_T} = \frac{1}{C_1} + \frac{1}{C_2}$$

For two capacitors in series, this simplifies to:

$$C_T = \frac{C_1 \times C_2}{C_1 + C_2}$$

(b) Capacitors in Parallel Connection

In parallel, the total charge is the sum of charges stored on each capacitor: \(Q_T = Q_1 + Q_2\).

Since \(Q = CV\):

$$C_T V_T = C_1 V_1 + C_2 V_2$$

Since potential difference across parallel branches is equal (\(V_T = V_1 = V_2 = V\)):

$$C_T V = V(C_1 + C_2) \implies C_T = C_1 + C_2$$

Factors Affecting Capacitance of a Parallel Plate Capacitor

  1. Area of the plates (\(A\)): Capacitance is directly proportional to plate area (\(C \propto A\)).
  2. Distance between the plates (\(d\)): Capacitance is inversely proportional to plate separation (\(C \propto \frac{1}{d}\)).
  3. Dielectric material (\(\varepsilon\)): Using a dielectric material like glass, mica, or polythene instead of air increases capacitance.
$$C = \frac{\varepsilon A}{d}$$

where \(\varepsilon\) is the permittivity of the dielectric medium.

1.6: Charge Distribution on a Conductor

  • Conductors can be spherical, pear-shaped, or cylindrical. Charge distribution behavior shows:
    • Hollow conductors carry charge exclusively on their outer surface.
    • Inside the solid body of a hollow conductor, net electrostatic charge is zero.
    • Charges concentrate heavily at sharp points or regions of high curvature where the local electric field strength is highest.

1.7: Lightning and Lightning Conductors

Lightning

  • Lightning is a massive natural electrostatic discharge taking place between two clouds or between a cloud and the ground.

How Lightning Happens

  • Friction between air currents and water drops in clouds leaves cloud bases negatively charged and upper sections positively charged. The negative charge base induces a high positive charge on the ground below.

Why Lightning Strikes in a Zig-Zag Pattern

  • Lightning follows the path of least electrical resistance through ionized atmospheric air pockets, creating an irregular zig-zag channel.

Thunderstorm

  • Extreme heat along the lightning path causes rapid expansion and subsequent collapse of air channels, creating shock waves heard as thunder.

Lightning Conductor

  • A protective metal rod mounted atop structures connected to a thick copper tape leading to an earth plate buried deep underground.

Mode of Action

  • Pointed spikes at the top create an intense electric field that leaks ions into the air to neutralize cloud charges (action of points). If a strike occurs, it offers a zero-resistance path safely discharging current directly into the ground.

Earthing (Grounding)

  • The process of connecting charged electrical systems or conductors directly to the earth to safely drain away unwanted charges.

Applications of Static Electricity

  1. Electrostatic Paint Spraying: Positively charged paint droplets spread evenly onto negatively charged vehicle bodies.
  2. Photocopying: Electrostatic charges attract toner powder to high-contrast document images on a drum.
  3. Inkjet Printing: Charged plates deflect micro-droplets of ink precisely onto paper.
  4. Electrostatic Precipitators: Drains industrial smoke particles inside chimneys to minimize pollution.

Dangers of Electrostatics

  • Fuel friction inside delivery pipes creates static charges; static sparks can ignite volatile fuel vapors. Conductive bonding and metal fuel containers dissipate these risks.

TOPIC-02: CURRENT ELECTRICITY

2.1: The Concept of Current Electricity

  • Current Electricity: The study of electric charges in motion, or the continuous flow of electric charge through a conductor.

Electric Current

  • Electric current is the rate of flow of electric charge.
$$\text{Electric Current } (I) = \frac{\text{Quantity of charge } (Q)}{\text{Time } (t)} \implies I = \frac{Q}{t}$$
  • The SI unit of electric current is the Ampere (A).
  • Ampere (A): The steady current produced when one coulomb of charge passes through a cross-section of a conductor per second.
  • An Ammeter is used to measure electric current.
  • Other metric units of current include:
    • \(1\text{ mA} = 10^{-3}\text{ A}\)
    • \(1\ \mu\text{A} = 10^{-6}\text{ A}\)
    • \(1\text{ kA} = 1000\text{ A}\)

Coulomb (C)

  • The SI unit of quantity of electric charge. It is the charge transferred by a steady current of one ampere flowing for one second (\(Q = I \times t\)).

Uses of Current Electricity

  1. Cooking and heating appliances
  2. Lighting systems
  3. Telecommunications and modern electronics
  4. Industrial machinery power

Sources of Current Electricity

  • Chemical Cells/Batteries: Convert chemical energy into electrical energy.
  • Generators/Dynamos: Convert mechanical energy into electrical energy.

2.2: Simple Electric Circuits

A simple electric circuit consists of:

  • A source of electromotive force (e.g., battery or generator).
  • Conducting path connections (usually copper wires).
  • Electrical components (loads, switches, resistors, ammeters, voltmeters).

Example:

An electric current of \(0.12\text{ A}\) passes a point along a wire. How much electric charge passes this point in 1 minute?

Solution:
Given: \(I = 0.12\text{ A}\), \(t = 1\text{ minute} = 60\text{ seconds}\)
$$Q = I \times t = 0.12 \times 60 = 7.2\text{ C}$$

Voltage and Potential Difference

  • Electromotive Force (e.m.f.): Total energy supplied by a cell per unit charge passing around a complete circuit.
  • Potential Difference (p.d.): The amount of electrical energy converted into other forms of energy when one coulomb of charge flows between two points.
  • Volt (V): The potential difference between two points when one joule of work is done moving one coulomb of charge between them (\(1\text{ V} = 1\text{ J/C}\)).
  • Measured using a Voltmeter connected in parallel across components.
  • An Ammeter is connected in series, with red (+) terminals directed toward the positive terminal of the power supply.

Electrical Resistance

  • Resistance is the opposition offered by a material to the flow of electric current.
  • The SI unit of resistance is the Ohm (\(\Omega\)).
  • Ohm (\(\Omega\)): The resistance of a conductor through which a current of one ampere flows when a potential difference of one volt is applied across its ends.

Types of Resistors

  1. Fixed resistors
  2. Variable resistors (Rheostats)

Ohm’s Law

Ohm's Law states that:

“At constant temperature and other physical conditions, the electric current passing through a conductor is directly proportional to the potential difference across its ends.”

$$V \propto I \implies V = IR \quad \text{or} \quad R = \frac{V}{I}$$
Graphical Representation:

From the \(V\text{ vs }I\) graph, the gradient gives the electrical resistance: \(\text{Slope } (m) = \frac{\Delta V}{\Delta I} = R\).

Factors Affecting the Resistance of a Wire

  1. Length of conductor (\(L\)): Resistance increases directly with length (\(R \propto L\)).
  2. Cross-sectional Area (\(A\)): Resistance is inversely proportional to thickness (\(R \propto \frac{1}{A}\)).
  3. Temperature (\(T\)): For metallic conductors, resistance increases as temperature rises.
  4. Type of Material: Different conductors exhibit different resistivities (e.g., Nichrome wire has higher resistance than Copper wire of identical dimensions).

Combination of Resistors

1. Series Connection

Resistors connected end-to-end along a single path.

  • Total potential difference is additive: \(V_T = V_1 + V_2\).
  • Since current \(I\) remains constant throughout:
    $$I R_T = I R_1 + I R_2 \implies R_T = R_1 + R_2 + \dots + R_n$$
2. Parallel Connection

Resistors connected across common voltage nodes.

  • Total current splits into individual branches: \(I_T = I_1 + I_2\).
  • Voltage across each branch is identical: \(V_T = V_1 = V_2 = V\).
  • Applying Ohm's law:
    $$\frac{V}{R_T} = \frac{V}{R_1} + \frac{V}{R_2} \implies \frac{1}{R_T} = \frac{1}{R_1} + \frac{1}{R_2} + \dots + \frac{1}{R_n}$$
  • Special shortcut formula for two parallel resistors:
    $$R_T = \frac{R_1 \times R_2}{R_1 + R_2}$$

N.B: House wiring uses parallel connections so each load operates independently at supply voltage without interrupting other appliances if one fails.

Example Calculation:

Two resistors of \(10\ \Omega\) and \(50\ \Omega\) are connected between points A and B. Find equivalent resistance in (a) Series, (b) Parallel.

Solution:
(a) Series: \(R_T = 10 + 50 = 60\ \Omega\)
(b) Parallel: \(R_T = \frac{10 \times 50}{10 + 50} = \frac{500}{60} = 8.33\ \Omega\)

TOPIC-03: MAGNETISM

3.1: Concept of Magnetism

  • A magnet is a substance which attracts metals or magnetic materials.
  • Magnetism is the behavior shown by a magnet, the behavior of attracting metals (magnetic materials).
  • Magnetic dipoles: Are the two poles on a magnet which are equal and opposite to each other.
  • Magnetic poles: Are ends of a magnet which show the strongest attraction or repulsion power compared to the other parts of the magnet (i.e. North seeking pole N and South seeking pole S).
  • Whereby N is the north seeking pole while S is the south seeking pole.
  • Magnetic domains are minute regions in ferromagnetic materials with millions of atomic dipoles coupled together in a preferred direction.

NB: The strength of a magnet cannot be increased beyond a certain limit because when all magnetic domains have oriented in the same direction, no further magnetization is possible and the material is said to be saturated.

Magnetic and Non-Magnetic Materials

  • Magnetic materials are all materials that can be magnetized or attracted by a magnet. E.g. iron, steel, nickel, and cobalt.
  • Non-magnetic materials are all materials that cannot be magnetized or attracted by a magnet. E.g. brass, copper, tin, zinc, carbon, aluminium, wood, plastic, clothes, etc.

Properties of Magnets

  1. Magnets attract magnetic materials.
  2. Magnet possesses two poles: northern pole (N) and southern pole (S).
  3. The magnetic force is strongest near the poles of a magnet.
  4. Like magnetic poles repel but unlike magnetic poles attract.
  5. The magnetic force is an action-at-a-distance force.
  6. If a magnet is suspended freely, it always points in the north-south direction.

Types of Magnets

  1. Temporary magnets
  2. Permanent magnets
  3. Electromagnets

1. Temporary Magnet

  • Occurs when magnetic material is magnetized in an external magnetic field but loses its magnetism when the field is removed.

2. Permanent Magnet

  • Is a magnet that holds magnetic properties over a long period of time.
  • It is made from hard material such as steel.

3. Electromagnet

  • Is a magnet formed when direct current (DC) electric current is allowed to flow through a wire coil wrapped round a metal core.

Shapes of Magnets

Magnets come in different shapes, including:

  1. A bar magnet
  2. A horse-shoe magnet
  3. Disc magnet

Sizes of Magnets

Magnet also varies in size, including:

  • Tiny discs used in speakers
  • Giant magnets used in power generating plants
  • Largest magnet is perhaps the earth itself

Application of Magnets

Magnets are used in:

  1. Magnetic recording media:
    • VHS tapes, audio cassettes, floppy and hard discs for recording data on a thin magnetic coating.
  2. Common television and computer monitors
  3. Transformers:
    • Are used in power transmission and many electronic devices.
  4. Credit, Debit and ATM cards:
    • It uses magnetic ink to store information to contact an individual's financial institution and connect with their account.
  5. Speakers and microphones:
    • They use permanent magnets and current-carrying coils to convert electric energy into sound energy.
  6. Electric generator: It uses permanent magnets to convert mechanical energy to electrical energy.
  7. Electromagnets used in hospitals or steel works

3.2: Magnetisation and Demagnetisation

Magnetization

Is the process of making a magnet, OR is the process of aligning the domains of atoms in material in one direction so as to produce a net effect of attraction or repulsion.

NB:

  • Magnetic dipoles arrange themselves in groups called “Magnetic Domains”.
  • Materials which are possible to cause this alignment or can be magnetized are either ferromagnetic or paramagnetic.

Ferromagnetic Material

  • Is the material which can form permanent magnets.
  • E.g. steel, nickel and cobalt.

Paramagnetic Material

  • Is the material which can be temporarily magnetized.
  • E.g. aluminium and chromium.

Methods Used to Magnetize Materials

  1. By heating and hammering method
  2. By stroking method
  3. By electric (solenoid) method
  4. By induction

1. Heating or Vibration Method

  • When magnetic material is heated in presence of external magnetic field, the dipoles of magnetic material align, whereby in the presence of external magnetic field the atoms of magnetic materials start to move and eventually become aligned. Many natural magnetic materials start out as part of lava.

2. Stroking Method

  • The pole of a bar magnet is dragged along the bar from one end to the other, then the material becomes magnetized.

3. Electric (Solenoid) Method

  • When soft iron core is wound with turns of wire and current passed through it, it produces a magnetic field. This is an industrial way of making a magnet.
  • This magnet that acts like a bar magnet is called a Solenoid.

4. By Induction Method

This is a method of magnetizing an object by placing it in an external magnetic field.

NB: Magnetic induction is the process by which a piece of magnetic material becomes a magnet when it is near or touching a permanent magnet.

Demagnetization

  • Is the process of removing magnetism from a given substance or material, OR is the process of disturbing the domains of an atom in a magnetized material.

Methods Used to Demagnetize a Magnet

1. Heating Method

  • If a magnet is placed in the East – West direction and heated to a temperature above its Curie temperature, then the magnet loses its magnetism.
  • This is because the increase in temperature results in greater atomic vibration which consequently prevents the domains from being aligned in the same direction.

2. Hammering

  • The magnet is placed in an East – West direction and then repeatedly hit or stroked with a hammer jarring the domains.

3. Electrical Method

  • A magnet is placed in an alternating magnetic field and then drawn slowly from the circuit and then the magnetic field is slowly decreased to zero. This principle is used in commercial demagnetizers to erase credit cards, hard disks etc. (The solenoid must be set in an East – West direction).

Storage of Magnets

In order to maintain the magnetism in magnets for a long period of time, the following practices have to be observed:

  1. Store away from ferrous materials.
  2. Store magnets in pairs and using magnetic keepers to store them.
  3. Store away from heat.
  4. Store away from strong electric and magnetic fields.
  5. Store away from strong vibration or mechanical impacts which may make them brittle.

3.3: Magnetic Field of a Magnet

Is a space around a magnet where a magnetic force is experienced (felt), OR is the region around a magnet in which magnetic materials are attracted by the magnet.

A magnetic field is represented by lines of action of magnetic force called “field lines”. Field lines show the direction of the magnetic force, hence are illustrated using arrows below.

Magnetic Lines of Force (Field Lines)

  • Are the imaginary lines that cojoin the poles of a magnet indicating its magnetic field.
  • The lines of force point away from the North Pole of a magnet and towards the South Pole.

Properties of Magnetic Lines of Force

  1. Lines of force are continuous and will always form closed loops starting at North Pole and ending at the South Pole.
  2. Magnetic force is stronger where the lines are close together and weaker where they are far apart.
  3. Lines of force will never cross one another.
  4. Magnetic lines of force pass through all materials (magnetic and non-magnetic).
  5. Parallel magnetic lines of force travelling in the same direction repel each other while lines of force travelling in opposite directions attract each other.
  6. Magnetic lines of force always enter or leave a magnetic material at right angles (\(90^\circ\)) to the surface.

NB: A point where the net magnetic field is zero is called a neutral point.

Magnetic Shielding

  • Is the process of limiting the flow of magnetic fields between two locations by separating them with a barrier made of conductive material, OR is the process of limiting the penetration of magnetic fields into a region by redirecting the magnetic field lines through a material with a higher ability to be magnetized.

3.4: Earth’s Magnetic Field

Earth's Magnetism

Earth's Magnetism is the behavior of the earth to act as a magnet.

Causes of Earth's Magnetism

The earth's magnetism is caused by the induced currents in it due to rotation on its own axis.

Earth behaves as if it has a short bar magnet inside it. It is inclined at a small angle to its axis of rotation, with its South Pole pointing to the northern hemisphere. This is inferred from the fact that the compass points towards the true north only at certain places.

Whereby:
\(\text{Ne}\) = North hemisphere
\(\text{Se}\) = South hemisphere

Whereby:
\(\text{GE}\) = Geographic equator
\(\text{ME}\) = Magnetic Equator
\(\Theta\) = Angle of declination
\(\text{SMP}\) = South magnetic pole
\(\text{NMP}\) = North magnetic pole
\(\text{NGP}\) = North geographic pole
\(\text{SGP}\) = South geographic pole
\(\alpha\) = Angle of dip or angle of inclination

Geographic Equator

  • Is the imaginary line which divides the earth into Northern and Southern hemispheres.

Magnetic Equator

  • Is an imaginary line parallel to the equator where a magnetic needle has no dip.

Magnetic Meridian

  • Is the imaginary line joining the earth magnetic North Pole and South Pole.

Geographic Meridian

  • Is the imaginary line joining the earth North Pole and South Pole.

South Magnetic Pole (SMP)

  • Is the pole near the geographic south pole of magnet.

North Magnetic Pole (NMP)

  • Is the pole near the geographic north pole of magnet.

Angle of Declination (\(\Theta\))

  • Is the angle formed between the magnetic meridian and geographic meridian.

Angle of Dip or Angle of Inclination (\(\alpha\))

  • Is the angle between the earth surface and the earth's magnetic field.

NB:

  • The angle of dip and declination both vary from place to place.
  • The angle of inclination is measured by using a Dip needle.
  • The angle of dip at the magnetic poles of the earth is \(90^\circ\) while at the equator it is \(0^\circ\).

How to Locate the Position of the North Pole of a Bar Magnet

  • If a bar magnet is suspended so that it rotates freely, one pole of the magnet will always turn toward the north, with the opposite pole facing south.
  • The pole of the magnet that orients northward is called the north pole (N) while the opposite pole is called south pole (S).

Application of the Earth's Magnetic Field

  1. Used by map-readers for finding locations of different places.
  2. Gives useful information in the search for minerals.
  3. Satellites transmit information through earth's magnetic field to the earth surface.
  4. The earth's external magnetic field (magnetosphere) protects the earth from harmful emissions from the sun.
  5. The earth's magnetic field enables the compass needle to operate.

4.2: Moment of a Force

Moment of a Force is the product of the force applied and the perpendicular distance from a fixed point or pivot.

OR

Moment of a force is the turning effect of the force about a point.

$$\text{Moment of force} = \text{Force} \times \text{Perpendicular distance from pivot}$$ $$M = F \times d$$

The SI unit of moment of force is the Newton meter (\(\text{N m}\)).

NB:

  • The point where the object rotates after a turning force is applied is called the pivot or fulcrum.
  • Moment of force is applied in different activities such as opening bottle caps, opening doors, tightening nuts, etc.
  • The moment of a force depends on the following:
    • Size of a force
    • Perpendicular distance

Application of Turning Effect in Daily Life

  • It is applied by a hand to unscrew a stopper on a bottle.
  • It is applied by a spanner to unscrew a nut on a bolt.
  • It is applied when turning a steering wheel of a car.
  • A force is applied to a door knob and the door swings open about its hinge.
  • It is applied when closing and opening water taps.

This gives some reasons as to why:

  • It is easier to open a nut with a long spanner than with short spanner (this is due to the higher moment resulting from the increased perpendicular distance of the long spanner).
  • It is easier to open the cap of a bottle with a bottle opener than with your fingers (this is due to the additional perpendicular distance provided by the opener).
  • A knob on a door is placed as far as possible from the hinges (this is due to the added perpendicular distance from the hinges to the knob).
Example:

A line of action of a force of \(90\text{ N}\) acts at a perpendicular distance of \(2.5\text{ m}\) from a point. Find the moment of the force.

Solution:
Force applied, \(F = 90\text{ N}\)
Perpendicular distance, \(d = 2.5\text{ m}\)
$$\text{Moment } (M) = F \times d = 90\text{ N} \times 2.5\text{ m} = 225\text{ N m}$$

Principle of Moments

Consider the ruler below:

The Principle of Moments states that:

“When a body is in equilibrium, the sum of the anticlockwise moments about any point is equal to the sum of the clockwise moments.”

OR

“When a system is in equilibrium the sum of clockwise moments is equal to the sum of anti-clockwise moments.”

$$\text{Clockwise Moment (CM)} = \text{Anticlockwise Moment (AM)}$$ $$F_1 d_1 = F_2 d_2$$
Example:

$$\text{CM} = \text{AM} \implies m_1 \times g \times d_1 = m_2 \times g \times d_2$$ $$100 \times 10 \times 45 = w \times 10 \times 20$$ $$100 \times 10 \times 45 = 200w$$ $$w = 225\text{ N} \quad (m = 22.5\text{ g})$$

4.3: Center of Gravity of a Body

  • The weight of a body is due to the attraction of the earth for its particles.
  • This attracting force is the weight of each individual particle. Since the body consists of many particles, the total weight is the resultant of all the parallel forces acting on the individual particles.
  • For a rigid body, there is one point at which the resultant force appears to act; this point is known as the center of gravity (\(G\)) of the body.

The Center of Gravity:

  • Is the point through which the resultant of the weight of all the particles of the body acts.
  • OR Is the point where the force of gravity can be considered to act.
  • OR Is the point on a body along which all the weight of the body is likely to act.

Difference Between Centre of Gravity and Centre of Mass

Centre of Gravity Centre of Mass
Is the point at which the whole weight of the body is likely to act. Is the point at which the whole mass of the body is assumed to be concentrated.
Weight distribution of the body around centre of gravity is uniform. The mass distribution around the centre of mass is uniform.
It changes with the change in the force of gravity. It remains unchanged with the change in the gravitational field.

Couple

A couple is a pair of equal but opposite parallel forces applied to the same body but not acting along the same line.

Characteristics of a Couple

  1. Comprises a pair of forces.
  2. The forces must be equal in magnitude.
  3. The forces must be parallel.
  4. The forces must act in opposite directions.

Moment (Torque) of a Couple

Is the product of one of the anti-parallel forces, \(F\), by the perpendicular distance between them.

Equilibrium

Equilibrium is a state achieved by a body when all the forces which act upon it are balanced.

OR Is the state of a body to balance.

NB: The force which brings a body into mechanical equilibrium is called the "Equilibrant".

Conditions for a Body to be in Equilibrium

  1. The net force on the object must be zero.
  2. The system (body) must have an acceleration of \(0\text{ m/s}^2\).

OR (also stated as):

  1. The sum of the forces in one direction must be equal to the sum of the forces in the opposite direction.
  2. The sum of clockwise moments should be equal to the sum of anticlockwise moments.

Conditions for Equilibrium Depend on Three Factors

  1. Magnitude
  2. Direction of forces
  3. Point of application

Factors Affecting the Stability of a Body

A stable body should have:

  1. Wide base
  2. Low centre of gravity

4.4: Types of Equilibrium

  1. Stable equilibrium
  2. Unstable equilibrium
  3. Neutral equilibrium

1. Stable Equilibrium

This occurs when a body is slightly displaced and then returns to its original position after displacement.

2. Unstable Equilibrium

It occurs when a body is slightly displaced and the body does not return to its original position after displacement.

3. Neutral Equilibrium

This occurs when a body is slightly displaced and the body does not alter the position of the center of gravity.

Application of Equilibrium

  1. Vehicles used in car races have tyres wide apart to increase stability.
  2. Cambered wheels: The wheels of a car are slightly tilted to increase stability so that when a car tilts to the side, it only skids.
  3. Used in designing structures like bridges, aeroplanes, furniture, machines, raceboats, ships, etc.
  4. Our body muscles are always in a kind of equilibrium, which enables us to walk, sit, eat, run, squat, jump, etc.
  5. Tall structures such as buildings and pylons have a wide base and low centre of gravity to provide stability.
  6. A bus or car with seated passengers and loading in the lower compartments is more stable than one with standing passengers and loads on top.
  7. Ships have long and wide projecting plates extending from their bases into the water to increase stability.
  8. Beam balance: Used for measuring masses of different objects by comparison with known masses.
  9. Steel yard: A machine used for weighing heavy objects utilizing the principle of moments by balancing heavy objects with lighter objects on a longer arm.
  10. Seesaw: A long plank balanced at the fulcrum so that an increase in weight on one side causes it to go down while the other side goes up.

5.1: Concept of Simple Machine

Machine

Machine is any device by means of which a force applied at one point can be used to overcome a force at some other point.

OR

Is any device which is used to simplify work.

OR

Is any device that uses energy to perform some activity (work) by making an applied force larger.

Examples are screw driver, pulley, inclined plane, bicycle, typewriter, car, etc.

Types of Machine

  1. Simple machine
  2. Complex machine

Simple Machine

Is any device that requires the application of a single force to do work.

OR

Is a mechanical device that helps to make work easier.

Examples are Screw driver, Crowbar, See-saw, Pulleys, Inclined plane, Hydraulic systems, Levers, wheel and axle, etc.

Complex Machine

A Complex Machine is a combination of more than one simple machine.

Examples are Bicycle, Typewriter, Car, Sewing machine, etc.

Terms Used

Effort (E)

Is a force which is applied to the machine so as to lift an object.

OR

Is the force used to operate a machine.

Its SI unit is Newton (\(\text{N}\)).

Load (L)

Is the resistance which a machine overcomes.

OR

Is the object to be moved from one place to another.

Its SI unit is Newton (\(\text{N}\)).

Mechanical Advantage (MA)

Mechanical advantage is the ratio of the load to the effort.

It has no SI unit.

$$\text{Mechanical Advantage} = \frac{\text{Load}}{\text{Effort}}$$ $$\text{M.A} = \frac{L}{E}$$
Example:

A man of mass \(100\text{ kg}\) lifts a box weight \(500\text{ kg}\) by standing on one end of a lever. How much mechanical advantage did the lever provide to the man while lifting the box?

Solution:
Given: Effort, \(E = 100\text{ kg} = 1000\text{ N}\), Load, \(L = 500\text{ kg} = 5000\text{ N}\)
Mechanical advantage, \(\text{MA} = ?\)
$$\text{From: } \text{MA} = \frac{L}{E}$$ $$\text{MA} = \frac{5000}{1000} = 5$$

Velocity Ratio (VR)

  • Velocity ratio is the ratio of the distance moved by the effort to the distance moved by load.
$$\text{Velocity Ratio} = \frac{\text{Effort distance}}{\text{Load distance}}$$ $$\text{V.R} = \frac{E_d}{L_d}$$
  • Velocity ratio has no SI unit.
  • Velocity ratio is not affected by friction.
Example:

When a machine pressed by effort moved down a distance of \(100\text{ cm}\), while the load is raised through \(25\text{ cm}\) at the same time. Find the velocity ratio.

Solution:
Given:
Distance moved by effort, \(E_d = 100\text{ cm}\)
Distance moved by load, \(L_d = 25\text{ cm}\)
Required: Velocity ratio, \(\text{VR} = ?$
$$\text{From: } \text{VR} = \frac{E_d}{L_d} = \frac{100}{25} = 4$$

Work Output (\(W_o\))

Work output is the product of load and distance moved by the load.

$$\text{Work Output} = L \times L_d$$

Work Input (\(W_i\))

Is the product of effort and distance moved by the effort.

$$\text{Work Input} = E \times E_d$$

Efficiency of a Machine

Is the ratio of the useful work derived from a machine to the energy put into it.

OR

Is the ratio of the work output to the work input.

NB: This ratio is always expressed as a percentage i.e.

$$\text{Efficiency} = \frac{\text{Work output}}{\text{Work input}} \times 100\%$$ $$\text{Efficiency} = \frac{L \times L_d}{E \times E_d} \times 100\% = \left(\frac{L}{E}\right) \times \left(\frac{L_d}{E_d}\right) \times 100\%$$ $$\text{Efficiency} = \frac{\text{M.A}}{\text{V.R}} \times 100\%$$

NB: The mechanical efficiency of a machine is always less than \(100\%\). This is because some energy is lost as heat due to friction.

A frictionless (ideal/perfect) machine would have an efficiency of \(100\%\).

Example:

A machine having a velocity ratio of \(5\) requires \(600\text{ J}\) of work to raise a load of \(400\text{ N}\) if the load moved through a distance of \(0.5\text{ m}\), calculate the mechanical advantage and efficiency of the machine.

Solution:
Given:
Velocity ratio, \(\text{VR} = 5\)
Work input, \(W_i = 600\text{ J}\)
Load, \(L = 400\text{ N}\)
Load distance, \(L_d = 0.5\text{ m}\)
Mechanical Advantage, \(\text{M.A} = ?$
Efficiency, \(e = ?$
$$\text{From: } e = \frac{W_o}{W_i} \times 100\%$$ $$\text{Since } W_o = L \times L_d = 400 \times 0.5 = 200\text{ J}$$ $$\text{Efficiency} = \frac{200}{600} \times 100\% = 33.33\%$$ $$\text{Also: } \text{Efficiency} = \frac{\text{M.A}}{\text{V.R}} \times 100\%$$ $$33.33\% = \frac{\text{M.A}}{5} \times 100\% \implies \text{M.A} = 1.67$$

5.2: Levers

A lever is a rigid bar (board or rod) that is supported on a single point around which it rotates.

OR

A Lever is a rigid body which when in use turns about a fixed point.

A lever has three main parts, these are Fulcrum, Load arm and Effort arm.

  • The lever is used to lift heavy weights with the least effort (the longer the bar, the easier it is to lift the load).
  • The fixed point is called Pivot (Fulcrum).
  • Fulcrum is the fixed point about which the bar moves.
  • Examples of levers are wheelbarrow, a pair of scissors, a shovel, etc.

Classes of Levers

  • First class levers
  • Second class levers
  • Third class levers

1. First Class Levers

  • In a first-class lever, the pivot is located between the effort and the load.
  • Examples are crowbars, scissors, pliers, see-saws, etc.

2. Second Class Levers

  • In a second-class lever, the load is located between the pivot and the effort.
  • Examples are wheelbarrows, nutcrackers, bottle openers, etc.

3. Third Class Levers

  • In a third-class lever, the effort is applied between the pivot and the load.
  • Examples are shovel (spade), tweezers, fishing rod, tongs, etc.

Mechanical Advantage of Levers

Is the ratio of effort arm (\(E_a\)) to the load arm (\(L_a\)).

$$\text{MA} = \frac{E_a}{L_a}$$

NB:

  1. Effort arm (\(E_a\)) is a distance between fulcrum and effort.
  2. Load arm (\(L_a\)) is distance between fulcrum and load.
  3. MA of second class is greater than first class lever.
  4. MA of third class is less than first class lever.
Example:

A force of \(30\text{ N}\) is applied at one end of a crowbar and overcomes a resistance of \(150\text{ N}\) at the lid of a case. Find the mechanical advantage.

Solution:
Given: Effort, \(E = 30\text{ N}\), Load, \(L = 150\text{ N}\), Required: Mechanical Advantage, \(\text{M.A} = ?$
$$\text{From: } \text{M.A} = \frac{L}{E} = \frac{150}{30} = 5$$

Velocity Ratio of a Lever

$$\text{V.R} = \frac{E_d}{L_d}$$

Where: \(E_d = \text{Distance moved by effort}\), \(L_d = \text{Distance moved by load}\)

$$\text{Efficiency} = \frac{\text{Work output}}{\text{Work input}} \times 100\%$$

5.3: Pulleys

A pulley is a simple machine that consists of a wheel that rotates around an axis called an axle.

The tension in the rope is assumed to be the same at all points in an ideal pulley.

Types of Pulleys

  1. Single fixed pulley
  2. Single movable pulley
  3. Combination pulley
  4. The block and tackle pulley system

1. Single Fixed Pulley

A single fixed pulley has its axle mounted in a fixed position. A rope passes round a groove in the wheel.

It is commonly used to raise flags to the top of a flagpole and by builders to lift cement blocks.

For a Single Fixed Pulley:

  • Neglecting the weight of the rope and friction of the pulley, the tension (\(T\)) in the rope is equal to the effort (\(E\)), and the load (\(L\)) equals the effort applied: \(\text{Effort } (E) = \text{Tension } (T) = \text{Load } (L)\).
  • $$\text{M.A} = \frac{L}{E} = \frac{L}{L} = 1$$
  • The load and effort move the same distance: \(E_d = L_d\).
  • $$\text{V.R} = \frac{E_d}{L_d} = \frac{E_d}{E_d} = 1$$

2. Single Movable Pulley

It consists of one pulley which is free to move up and down with the load.

For a Single Movable Pulley:

$$\text{M.A} = \frac{L}{E}$$

The tension (\(T\)) in the string is equal to the effort applied, so the total upward pull on the pulley is twice the effort (\(2E\), where \(E = T\)). Therefore, the load \(L = 2T = 2E\).

$$\text{M.A} = \frac{L}{E} = \frac{2E}{E} = 2$$ $$\text{V.R} = \frac{\text{Distance moved by effort}}{\text{Distance moved by load}} = \frac{x}{\frac{1}{2}x} = 2$$

3. Combined Pulley System

It consists of a combination of fixed and movable pulleys.

$$\text{M.A} = \frac{L}{E}$$ $$\text{V.R} = n \quad (\text{where } n \text{ is the number of pulleys or rope segments supporting the load})$$

4. The Block and Tackle Pulley System

It is a combination pulley that consists of several fixed and movable pulleys arranged in two blocks. It is widely used in sailing ships to lift heavy sails and in garages to lift car engines.

Uses of Pulleys in Daily Life

  • Applicable in winches for building works.
  • Used as transport systems up mountain slopes.
  • Used by fishermen to lift heavy loads or move boats.
  • Used in garages to lift car engines.
  • Used for fetching water in wells.

5.4: Inclined Plane

An inclined plane is a smooth flat rigid surface slanted at an angle to the horizontal. Examples of inclined planes include staircases, winding roads, and ramps.

Whereby:

  • Vertical height = Load distance (\(L_d\))
  • Slanted height = Effort distance (\(E_d\))
$$\text{M.A} = \frac{L}{E}, \quad \text{V.R} = \frac{E_d}{L_d}$$ $$\text{Since } \sin\theta = \frac{\text{Load distance}}{\text{Effort distance}} = \frac{L_d}{E_d} \implies \text{V.R} = \frac{1}{\sin\theta}$$ $$\text{Efficiency} = \frac{\text{M.A}}{\text{V.R}} \times 100\%$$
Example:

A force of \(600\text{ N}\) is used to move a load of \(3000\text{ N}\) up an inclined plane. Given that the slanted height and vertical height of the plane are \(18\text{ m}\) and \(3\text{ m}\) respectively. Find:

(a) Velocity ratio of the plane
(b) Mechanical advantage
(c) Efficiency of the plane

Solution:
Given: \(E = 600\text{ N}\), \(L = 3000\text{ N}\), \(E_d = 18\text{ m}\), \(L_d = 3\text{ m}\)
(a) \(\text{V.R} = \frac{E_d}{L_d} = \frac{18}{3} = 6$
(b) \(\text{M.A} = \frac{L}{E} = \frac{3000}{600} = 5$
(c) \(\text{Efficiency} = \frac{\text{M.A}}{\text{V.R}} \times 100\% = \frac{5}{6} \times 100\% = 83.3\%\)

5.5: Screw

A screw is a form of an inclined plane in which the plane is wrapped around a cylindrical rod. A screw jack consists of a threaded rod operated by a lever arm.

  • The distance between two successive threads is referred to as the pitch (\(p\)).
  • The mechanical advantage of a screw jack depends on its pitch and the length of the effort handle.
$$\text{Pitch} = \frac{\text{Length of the threaded rod}}{\text{Number of threads}}$$ $$\text{V.R} = \frac{\text{Circumference of the effort circle}}{\text{Pitch}} = \frac{2\pi R}{p}$$

5.6: Wheel And Axle

A wheel and axle consists of a wheel and an axle mounted and joined together so they have the same axis of rotation.

Whereby:

  • Circumference of axle = Load distance (\(L_d\))
  • Circumference of wheel = Effort distance (\(E_d\))
  • Radius of wheel = \(R\), Radius of axle = \(r\)

For a Wheel and Axle:

$$\text{M.A} = \frac{L}{E}$$ $$\text{V.R} = \frac{E_d}{L_d} = \frac{2\pi R}{2\pi r} = \frac{R}{r}$$ $$\text{Efficiency} = \frac{\text{M.A}}{\text{V.R}} \times 100\%$$
Example:

(a) \(5\text{ cm}\)     (b) \(8\text{ cm}\)     (c) \(12\text{ cm}\)

Solution:
Given: Velocity ratio, \(\text{VR} = 6\), Radius of the wheel, \(R = ?$
$$\text{Since } \text{V.R} = \frac{R}{r} \implies R = \text{V.R} \times r$$ (a) If radius of axle, \(r = 5\text{ cm}\):
$$R = 6 \times 5 = 30\text{ cm}$$ (b) If radius of axle, \(r = 8\text{ cm}\):
$$R = 6 \times 8 = 48\text{ cm}$$ (c) If radius of axle, \(r = 12\text{ cm}\):
$$R = 6 \times 12 = 72\text{ cm}$$

5.7: Hydraulic Press

A hydraulic press multiplies an applied effort using the pressure of a liquid or gas. This allows the lifting of a heavy load by applying a small effort.

Assume there is no frictional force:

By the principle of transmission of pressure in liquids, the pressure on the effort piston equals that on the load piston.

$$P_1 = P_2, \quad \text{Since } P = \frac{F}{A}$$ $$\text{Whereby: } F_1 = E = P_1 A_1, \quad F_2 = L = P_2 A_2$$ $$\text{Since } \text{M.A} = \frac{L}{E} \implies \text{M.A} = \frac{A_2}{A_1}$$ $$\text{Whereby: } A_2 = \pi R^2 \quad \text{and} \quad A_1 = \pi r^2$$ $$\text{M.A} = \frac{R^2}{r^2}$$

In practical cases, the mechanical advantage is less than \(\frac{R^2}{r^2}\) due to frictional forces in the hydraulic press. Therefore, the efficiency of a hydraulic press is less than \(100\%\).

Velocity Ratio of a Hydraulic Press

Assuming that the hydraulic press is frictionless, then:

$$\text{Work output} = \text{Work input}$$ $$L \times L_d = E \times E_d$$ $$\frac{L}{E} = \frac{E_d}{L_d}$$ $$\text{But } \text{M.A} = \frac{L}{E} = \frac{R^2}{r^2}$$ $$\therefore \text{V.R} = \text{M.A} = \frac{R^2}{r^2} \quad (\text{If the hydraulic press is } 100\% \text{ efficient})$$
Example:

The diagram below shows a hydraulic press being used to lift a container weighting \(100000\text{ N}\).

Radii of the effort and load pistons are \(20\text{ cm}\) and \(5\text{ m}\) respectively. If the efficiency of the hydraulic press is \(90\%\), determine:

(a) Velocity ratio
(b) Mechanical advantage
(c) Minimum Effort
(d) The distance the container is raised through if the effort piston is pushed through \(1\text{ m}\)

Solution:
Given: \(L = 100000\text{ N}\), Radius of Load piston \(R = 5\text{ m}\), Radius of effort piston \(r = 20\text{ cm} = 0.2\text{ m}\), \(\text{Efficiency} = 90\%\)
(a) \(\text{V.R} = \frac{R^2}{r^2} = \frac{5^2}{0.2^2} = \frac{25}{0.04} = 625\)
(b) \(\text{Efficiency} = \frac{\text{M.A}}{\text{V.R}} \times 100\% \implies \text{M.A} = \frac{90 \times 625}{100} = 562.5\)
(c) \(\text{From: } \text{M.A} = \frac{L}{E} \implies E = \frac{L}{\text{M.A}} = \frac{100000}{562.5} = 117.78\text{ N}\)
(d) \(\text{V.R} = \frac{E_d}{L_d} \implies L_d = \frac{E_d}{\text{V.R}} = \frac{1}{625} = 0.0016\text{ m}\)

6.1: Concept of Motion

Motion:
Motion is the process of continuously changing in position of an object from one place to another.

Types of Motion

  • Linear motion
  • Circular motion

Linear Motion

  • Is a motion of an object in a straight line.

Circular Motion

  • Is a motion of an object around the circle.
  • Example of circular motion: Rotation of the earth and motion of electrons around the nucleus

Terms used to describe Motion

  • Distance and displacement
  • Speed and velocity
  • Acceleration and Retardation

Distance and displacement

Distance

  • Is the length of path taken by an object in motion.
  • Distance is represented by letter \(s\)
  • The distance is a scalar quantity.
  • The SI unit of distance is Metre (\(\text{m}\)). Other units are Centimeter (\(\text{cm}\)), and Kilometer (\(\text{km}\))

6.2: Speed and Velocity

Speed

Is the distance moved per unit time.
OR
Is the rate of change of distance.

The speed is represented by letter \(v\).
It is a scalar quantity.

$$\text{speed}(v) = \frac{\text{distance}}{\text{Time taken}} = \frac{s}{t}$$
  • The SI unit of speed is Meter per Second (\(\text{m/s}\)). Other unit used is kilometer per hour (\(\text{km/h}\))

Velocity

  • Is the rate of change of displacement
  • It is a vector quantity.
  • $$\text{Velocity}(v) = \frac{\text{displacement}}{\text{Time taken}} = \frac{s}{t}$$
  • The SI unit of velocity is Meter per Second (\(\text{m/s}\)). Other units used is kilometer per hour (\(\text{km/h}\))
  • NB: \(10\text{ m/s} = 36\text{ km/h}\)

NB:

  • Initial velocity is the velocity of the body at the starting point of observation.
  • Final velocity is the velocity of the body at the ending point of observation.
  • Average velocity is the mean of initial and final velocities
    OR
    Average velocity is the ratio of the total displacement to the total time.
    $$\text{Average velocity}(V_a) = \frac{s}{t}$$
  • Uniform velocity is the type of velocity in which the rate of change of displacement with time is constant.
  • Instantaneous velocity is the velocity of the body at any instant.
Example:

An object travelled \(20\text{ m}\) to the right in \(4\text{ s}\) and then \(12\text{ m}\) to the left in \(3\text{ s}\), for its total motion. What was its average speed & its average velocity?

Data given:
Total distance traveled, \(s = 20\text{ m} + 12\text{ m} = 32\text{ m}\)
Total time, \(t = 4\text{ s} + 3\text{ s} = 7\text{ s}\)

$$\text{Average speed} = \frac{\text{total distance}}{\text{total time}} = \frac{32}{7} = 4.57\text{ m/s}$$

When the object is moving to right its displacement is positive and when to the left its displacement is negative
Total displacement (\(s\)) \(= 20 + (-12)\text{ m} = 8\text{ m}\)

$$\text{Average velocity} = \frac{\text{total displacement}}{\text{total time}} = \frac{8}{7} = 1.14\text{ m/s}$$

6.3: Acceleration

Acceleration

  • Is the rate of change of velocity. It is denoted by small letter “a”
    $$\text{acceleration} = \frac{\text{final velocity} - \text{initial velocity}}{\text{time}}, \quad a = \frac{v - u}{t}$$
  • Its SI unit is metre per second square (\(\text{m/s}^2\))
  • Uniform acceleration is the type of acceleration in which the rate of change of velocity is CONSTANT.

Retardation (Deceleration)

  • Is the rate of decreasing of velocity.
  • It is referred as negative acceleration.
  • Uniform retardation is the one in which the rate of decreasing of velocity does not change.

NB:

  • When a body starts moving from rest its initial velocity become zero, \(u = 0\text{ m/s}\)
  • When a body is brought to rest by the application of brakes its final velocity, \(v = 0\text{ m/s}\)
  • When a velocity of a moving object increases its acceleration become positive
  • When the velocity of a moving object decreases its acceleration become negative
  • When a body is moving with a uniform velocity its acceleration becomes zero, \(a = 0\text{ m/s}^2\)
Example:

Final velocity, \(v = -5\text{ m/s}\)
Time taken, \(t = 8\text{ s}\)
Acceleration, \(a = ?\)

$$\text{From } a = \frac{v - u}{t} = \frac{-5 - 15}{8} = \frac{-20}{8} = -2.5\text{ m/s}^2$$

6.4: Equations of Linear Motion

  • First equation of motion
  • Second equation of motion
  • Third equation of motion

Consider a body moving with a constant acceleration \(a\) from an initial velocity \(u\), to a final velocity \(v\). The body covers a displacement, \(s\), after sometime \(t\).

Derivation of first equation

Now, find the acceleration of the body
From; \(a = \frac{\Delta v}{t} = \frac{v - u}{t}\)
\(at = v - u \implies v = u + at\)

$$\therefore \text{The } 1^{\text{st}} \text{ equation is given by } v = u + at$$

Derivation of the second equation

Find the average velocity of the body
\(V_a = \frac{u + v}{2} = \frac{s}{t} \implies s = \frac{(u + v)t}{2}\)
But \(v = u + at\), Then; \(s = \frac{(u + u + at)t}{2}\)
\(\implies s = \frac{(2u + at)t}{2}\), Then; \(s = \frac{2ut + at^2}{2}\)
\(\implies s = ut + \frac{1}{2}at^2\)

$$\therefore \text{The } 2^{\text{nd}} \text{ equation is given by; } s = ut + \frac{1}{2}at^2$$

Derivation of third equation

From; \(s = ut + \frac{1}{2}at^2\)
Consider the \(1^{\text{st}}\) equation; \(v = u + at\)
Then square the equation in each side
\(v^2 = (u + at)^2\)
\(v^2 = u^2 + 2uat + a^2t^2\)
\(v^2 = u^2 + 2a\left(ut + \frac{1}{2}at^2\right)\)
But; \(ut + \frac{1}{2}at^2 = s\)
\(\implies v^2 = u^2 + 2as\)

$$\therefore \text{The } 3^{\text{rd}} \text{ equation is given by; } v^2 = u^2 + 2as$$
Example:

A body moving with a velocity of \(30\text{ m/s}\) is accelerated uniformly to a velocity of \(50\text{ m/s}\) in \(5\text{ s}\). Calculate the acceleration and the distance traveled by the body.

Data given:
Initial velocity (\(u\)) \(= 30\text{ m/s}\), Final velocity (\(v\)) \(= 50\text{ m/s}\), Time (\(t\)) \(= 5\text{ s}\), Acceleration \(= ?\)

$$a = \frac{v - u}{t} = \frac{50 - 30}{5} = 4\text{ m/s}^2$$

Distance traveled \(= ?\)
\(s = ut + \frac{1}{2}at^2\)
\(s = 30 \times 5 + \frac{1}{2} \times 4 \times 5^2 = 125\text{ m}\)

6.5: Motion Under Gravity

  • All bodies on the earth will always fall down towards the earth's surface when released from a point. What makes these bodies falling downwards is the acceleration of free falling body called acceleration due to gravity which is \(9.8\text{ m/s}^2\) or \(10\text{ N/kg}\).
  • Acceleration of free falling body is denoted by '\(g\)'. Light bodies like feathers, paper etc are observed to fall down more slowly than iron balls. This is because light bodies are very much affected by air resistance.

There are two important characteristics of free fall

  1. Free falling objects do not encounter air resistance
  2. All free falling objects on earth accelerate downwards at a rate of \(9.8\text{ m/s}^2\) (often approximated as \(10\text{ m/s}^2\))

For a body moving downwards the following formulae are applied (Here \(a = g\) and \(s = h\)):

$$\text{The } 1^{\text{st}} \text{ equation is given by; } v = u + gt$$ $$\text{The } 2^{\text{nd}} \text{ equation is given by; } h = ut + \frac{1}{2}gt^2$$ $$\text{The } 3^{\text{rd}} \text{ equation is given by; } v^2 = u^2 + 2gh$$

When the body moves upwards, the formulae will change to: (Here; \(a = -g\) and \(s = h\))

$$\text{The } 1^{\text{st}} \text{ equation is given by; } v = u - gt$$ $$\text{The } 2^{\text{nd}} \text{ equation is given by; } h = ut - \frac{1}{2}gt^2$$ $$\text{The } 3^{\text{rd}} \text{ equation is given by; } v^2 = u^2 - 2gh$$
Example:

A stone is thrown vertically upward from the ground with a velocity of \(30\text{ m/s}\). Find:

  1. Maximum height reached
  2. Time taken for maximum height
  3. Time taken for reach ground again
  4. The velocity reached half-way to the maximum height

Solution:

(a) Maximum height reached, \(s = H = ?\)
Data given:
Initial velocity, \(u = 30\text{ m/s}\), Final velocity, \(v = 0\text{ m/s}\), Acceleration, \(a = -g = -10\text{ m/s}^2\)

From: Third equation of motion
\(v^2 = u^2 - 2gh\)
Make \(h\) the subject:
$$h = \frac{u^2 - v^2}{2gh} \implies h = \frac{30^2 - 0^2}{2 \times 10} = \frac{900}{20} = 45\text{ m}$$

(b) Time taken for maximum height, \(t = ?\)
\(v = u - gt \implies t = \frac{u - v}{g} = \frac{30 - 0}{10} = 3\text{ s}\)

(c) Time taken for reach ground again, (\(T = 2t\)) \(= ?\)
$$T = 2t = 2 \times 3 = 6\text{ sec}$$

(d) Velocity reached halfway to the maximum height, \(v = ?\)
When the stone is halfway to maximum height, the height attained is:
\(h = \frac{H}{2} = \frac{45}{2} = 22.5\text{ m}\)
\(v^2 = u^2 - 2gh\)
$$v = \sqrt{u^2 - 2gh} = \sqrt{30^2 - 2 \times 10 \times 22.5} = \sqrt{900 - 450} = \sqrt{450} = 21.2\text{ m/s}$$
